\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Open-NL} is an open-source computational testbed designed for
the transparent modeling, component-level ablation, and evaluation of
Wide Dynamic Range Compression (WDRC) prescriptive rules.
\textbf{Crucially, Open-NL is strictly a hypothesis-generating
algorithmic sandbox, not a deployable clinical formula; its outputs must
not be used on human patients, and the resulting intelligibility and
loudness data are presented solely as mechanistic demonstrations of
optimizer behavior, not comparative efficacy claims.} While modern
prescriptions like NAL-NL2 rely on extensive empirical regularizations
to ensure clinical safety---historically necessitated by the
well-documented failures of theoretical
intelligibility-maximization---their closed-source, compiled nature
prevents researchers from isolating the interactions of specific
heuristic safeguards. Open-NL fills this gap by coupling a Nelder-Mead
effective (desensitized) Speech Intelligibility Index (SII) optimizer
with the Moore \& Glasberg (2004) specific-loudness model within an
established 7-profile evaluation paradigm. We benchmark this testbed
across a \(4^4 = 256\)-permutation heuristic sweep alongside a 768-point
Monte Carlo stability analysis, revealing a fundamental distinction
between numerical convergence and heuristic sensitivity: while the
multi-start Nelder-Mead solver exhibits high numerical convergence
stability for any fixed parameter set (\(\text{SD} < 0.025\) SII), the
prescribed targets exhibit extreme heuristic parameter sensitivity for
severe-sloping configurations, swinging modeled monaural loudness
between 0.9 and 5.3 sones when shifting minor heuristic boundaries.
Crucially, because standard models underestimate real-world binaural
broadband summation by \textasciitilde2.4\(\times\) in listeners with
excess summation, this modeled loudness frontier is strictly
illustrative rather than clinically definitive. Furthermore, Open-NL's
mathematically optimal high-frequency targets for profound profiles
dramatically exceed standard clinical baselines, establishing a formally
testable hypothesis that uncalibrated limits will systematically trigger
\textgreater80\% behavioral loudness rejection due to unmodeled
broadband excess summation. By providing an open-source, inspectable
framework that quantitatively exposes these trade-offs, Open-NL provides
the foundational computational substrate for future stochastic
calibration roadmaps (GA/PSO) designed to replace static heuristic
boundaries with individualized, distortion-aware objective functions.

\subsection{I. INTRODUCTION}\label{i.-introduction}

Manufacturer-agnostic prescriptions remain central to evidence-based
hearing aid practice. While some earlier studies suggested these generic
targets might outperform proprietary first-fit algorithms on patient
preference and specific metrics (Valente et al., 2018), contemporary
data demonstrates that measured speech recognition often shows no
significant difference between formulas. Yet, while formula choice has
relatively modest intelligibility consequences in background noise, it
drives massive variations in overall loudness, making loudness
(quantified in sones per the Moore \& Glasberg 2004 impaired loudness
model) the most informative dependent variable in prescriptive
evaluation.

While the derivations and optimization objectives of major algorithms
like NAL-NL2 and DSL m{[}i/o{]} are published in detail, their compiled
software implementations are closed-source. Audiological science has
long recognized that theoretical intelligibility-maximization fails
clinically without extensive empirical regularization. The historical
evolution from NAL-NL1 to NAL-NL2 specifically required empirical
corrections---including global gain reductions (e.g., -2 dB for female
listeners and new users) and reduced compression ratios for severe
losses---to counteract the aggressive over-amplification provoked by
unconstrained optimization (Keidser, Dillon, Carter, \& O'Brien, 2012).
However, because modern fitting software packages are closed-source,
researchers cannot isolate the relative contribution of individual
heuristic rules or observe how specific safeguards interact within the
optimization cascade. Existing open-source frameworks provide
significant utility but serve distinct functional niches: openMHA
(Herzke et al., 2017) operates as a real-time signal processing master
hearing aid rather than a prescriptive target generator, the Cambridge
CAM2/CAMEQ2-HF formulae (Moore et al., 2010) provide rigidly defined
equation-based targets rather than a modular optimization sandbox, and
the Auditory Modeling Toolbox (AMT; Majdak et al., 2022) offers robust
loudness integrations without native prescriptive inversion.
Consequently, investigators cannot isolate a specific prescriptive rule
within a modern optimization loop and observe the resulting cascade
without reverse-engineering an entire proprietary engine. Open-NL fills
this defensible gap by providing a modifiable R substrate explicitly
designed for the modular ablation of prescriptive heuristics. By
coupling a Nelder-Mead effective SII optimizer to an integrated C++
specific-loudness engine, this framework permits researchers to
systematically disable, isolate, or invert individual heuristics. For
example, a researcher could seamlessly disable the traditional 30 dB
safety cap for mixed losses, allowing the optimizer to aggressively
target full mechanical restoration, and then evaluate the resulting
theoretical upward spread of masking---a specific topological hypothesis
that a rigid clinical formula or a real-time audio processor cannot
natively evaluate. The clinical utility of algorithmic transparency and
alternative, modular rule sets is increasingly recognized; for example,
the evolving NAL-NL3 protocol explicitly introduces alternative
philosophical profiles designed for specific situational or cohort needs
(Kitterick et al., 2026).

Open-NL provides a parameterized, inspectable research testbed
conceptually aligned with this evolving philosophy of modular
optimization. It is designed as a modular substrate permitting
component-level ablation of prescriptive rules, where individual
heuristic components---such as slope-dependent penalties or severe-loss
boosters---can be independently toggled and their modeled consequences
directly measured.

Crucially, to benchmark this testbed without introducing conflating
variables, the optimization layer is embedded within a strictly
reproduced evaluation paradigm. The seven reference audiometric
profiles, the Moore \& Glasberg (2004) specific loudness model, and the
ANSI S3.5 SII metric utilized herein are a direct replication of the
methodological framework established by Johnson and Dillon (2011).
(Note: Throughout this manuscript, ``ANSI SII'' refers to the raw
physical audibility calculation, whereas ``effective SII'' or
``desensitized SII'' refers to the calculation incorporating severe-loss
desensitization and level distortion penalties). Because this underlying
physiological evaluation space is already established in the literature,
the primary scientific contribution of this manuscript is the
transparent, open-source computational testbed itself. By exposing the
behavior of unconstrained numerical solvers within this standardized
sandbox, the paper clarifies a crucial distinction: while numerical
solvers converge stably on any fixed objective space, theoretical WDRC
target generation exhibits profound parameter sensitivity to
uncalibrated heuristic boundaries, providing the computational
infrastructure necessary to quantify and calibrate these interactions.

\subsubsection{Clinical Safety and Usage
Disclaimer}\label{clinical-safety-and-usage-disclaimer}

It is imperative to state unambiguously that Open-NL is strictly a
computational research testbed and \textbf{must not be used for fitting
hearing aids on human listeners in its current form}. Because the
framework deliberately permits aggressive, over-prescriptive targets for
boundary testing---such as utilizing an aggressive 60 dB HL severe-loss
booster onset that permits 14--15 sones of theoretical loudness for
profound losses---it carries a significant risk of severe
over-amplification. As established by Ching, Dillon, Katsch, and Byrne
(2001), aggressive high-level targets in steeply sloping or profound
losses are precisely where over-amplification risks are greatest, as the
effectiveness of high-frequency audibility severely degrades as hearing
loss worsens (desensitization). The hypotheses and targets generated by
Open-NL represent extreme mathematical boundaries intended to trigger
experimental loudness rejection in controlled research settings, not
clinical solutions. Any future behavioral translation of this framework
requires independent institutional review, with mandatory real-ear
verification and strict, individualized loudness-tolerance limits
implemented as absolute prerequisites.

\subsection{II. ALGORITHM
ARCHITECTURE}\label{ii.-algorithm-architecture}

\subsubsection{A. Prescriptive Rationale and Objective
Function}\label{a.-prescriptive-rationale-and-objective-function}

The choice of objective function represents the deepest design decision
in any prescriptive formula, establishing the foundational compromise
between intelligibility and comfort. Historically, established
rationales occupy distinct theoretical positions on this spectrum:
NAL-NL2 maximizes speech intelligibility while strictly constraining
overall broadband loudness to be less than or equal to that of a
normal-hearing listener (Keidser, Dillon, Carter, \& O'Brien, 2012); DSL
m{[}i/o{]} normalizes loudness at each frequency to restore normal
perception across the dynamic range (Scollie et al., 2005); and
CAMEQ/CAM2 aim to equalize loudness across varying frequency bands
(Moore, Glasberg, \& Stone, 2010).

Open-NL explicitly positions its prescriptive rationale as a
\emph{constrained intelligibility-maximizer}. The core mathematical
objective is the unconstrained maximization of the effective
(desensitized) Speech Intelligibility Index (SII). However, rather than
globally bounding this maximization to a static ``normal-or-less''
loudness limit, Open-NL permits loudness to grow dynamically until it
strikes a hard, U-shaped physiological ceiling (controlled via the
\texttt{cap\_knots} parameter; see Section II.K). This U-shaped penalty
structure specifically permits slightly higher-than-normal loudness in
the mid-frequencies for severe losses---where the intelligibility yield
is highest---while aggressively decelerating loudness growth at the
spectral extremes.

This specific constraint topology is designed to explore the boundary
between intelligibility maximization and loudness limiting. However, it
must be stated prominently that this U-shaped penalty operates strictly
within the canonical Moore \& Glasberg (2004) monaural specific-loudness
engine. While the heuristic dynamically restricts modeled monaural
sones, it does not---and mathematically cannot---account for the
idiosyncratic binaural broadband loudness summation observed in
hearing-impaired listeners. As demonstrated by Moore et al.~(2014),
Oetting et al.~(2016), and Denk et al.~(2025), binaural broadband
summation in impaired populations averages \textasciitilde13 dB higher
than in normal-hearing listeners (a factor of \(\approx 2.4\times\) in
linear sones), with roughly 30--40\% exceeding the normal range
entirely. Because standard monaural and narrowband models cannot predict
this broadband excess summation from the pure-tone audiogram, Open-NL's
U-shaped constraint serves strictly as an exploratory, illustrative
mathematical ceiling rather than a guaranteed physiological safety
barrier for bilateral fittings. By executing unconstrained SII
maximization beneath this localized, U-shaped monaural penalty, Open-NL
illustrates the theoretical trade-offs of pushing localized loudness
beyond normal boundaries, while exposing the fundamental clinical
vulnerability of relying on uncalibrated monaural loudness models.

\subsubsection{C. Algorithmic Pipeline and Execution
Cascade}\label{c.-algorithmic-pipeline-and-execution-cascade}

The internal execution cascade of Open-NL is structured into twelve
strictly ordered, modular processing stages: 1. \textbf{Conductive
Component Separation \& Baseline Partitioning}: Decomposing raw
audiometric thresholds into sensorineural components and air-bone gaps
(ABGs). 2. \textbf{Decoupled Half-Gain Base Anchor Calculation}:
Establishing a frequency-specific base gain anchor
(\(G_{base} = 0.46 \cdot \text{HTL}_{sn} + C_{interp}\)) decoupled from
broadband PTA. 3. \textbf{Experience-Level Shaping \& Log-Frequency
\(C_{vals}\) Interpolation}: Modulating frequency-shaping arrays across
discrete anchor frequencies based on listener experience (new,
experienced, power). 4. \textbf{Reverse-Slope Low-Frequency Attenuation
Floor}: Applying a bounded log-linear low-frequency taper down toward a
\(-10\) dB floor when low frequencies are severely worse than high
frequencies. 5. \textbf{Slope-Dependent Low-Frequency Penalty (SD-LFP)
with Profound HF Bypass}: Dynamically suppressing low-frequency gain for
steeply sloping losses, while gating the penalty when high-frequency
severity exceeds 70--95 dB HL to protect low-frequency speech cues. 6.
\textbf{Severe-Loss Audibility Booster}: Providing an opt-in, bounded
linear escalation (slope = 0.15) for severe thresholds. 7.
\textbf{Soft-Compression High-Frequency Desensitization}: Restricting
excessive high-frequency gain via a dynamic ceiling
(\(L_{gain} = 30 + 0.4 \cdot \max(0, \text{HTL}_{sn} - 60)\)) and 2:1
soft compression to combat dead-region distortion. 8. \textbf{Dynamic
Range Mapping (LDL Squeeze)}: Attenuating gain by 0.2 dB/dB of reduced
dynamic range and shifting baseline compression ratios. 9.
\textbf{Transducer Bandwidth Roll-off}: Applying a continuous
log-frequency piecewise multiplier (\(M_{bw} \in [0.5, 1.0]\)) to
suppress unstable edge frequencies (\(\le 250\) Hz and \(\ge 6000\) Hz).
10. \textbf{Multi-Channel WDRC Mapping, LTASS Pivot, and Demographic
Adjustments}: Calculating dynamic compression ratios, variable
compression thresholds, piecewise linear-compressive splines, and
demographic offsets (gender, unilateral fitting). 11. \textbf{Acoustic
Venting, Coupling Loss, and Receiver Saturation Limits}: Integrating
real-ear insertion loss vectors across 10 coupling types with a feedback
safety floor, and establishing MPO/SSPL90 receiver saturation limits
capped at 120 dB SPL. 12. \textbf{Embedded Nelder-Mead Simplex
Optimization \& Physiological Loudness Ceilings}: Iteratively refining
multi-level gain curves against a multi-term objective loss function
incorporating U-shaped Moore \& Glasberg (2004) specific-loudness caps,
compression ratio ceilings, and spectral smoothness constraints.

Because Open-NL is designed as an open-source testbed rather than a
proprietary black box, the exact mathematical formulations, closed-form
piecewise equations, and complete numerical parameter specifications
governing each of these twelve stages are provided in full detail in the
accompanying \textbf{Supplementary Material} document.

\subsubsection{O. Consolidated Constants and Evidentiary
Asymmetry}\label{o.-consolidated-constants-and-evidentiary-asymmetry}

Because Open-NL functions strictly as a modifiable computational testbed
rather than a clinically validated prescriptive formula, several
structural parameters remain mathematically uncalibrated. The algorithm
is structurally dominated by asserted constants: the 0.46 gain anchor,
0.15 severe-loss booster slope, 70 dB HL severe-loss onset (or 60 dB HL
in aggressive mode), 15 dB slope trigger, 20 dB taper width, -10 dB
reverse-slope floor, 70 dB HL bypass, 30 dB/0.4 \(L_{gain}\) interaction
constraint, the 0.2 dB/dB dynamic range compression squeeze, the 75\%
air-bone-gap restoration fraction, and the 1.5:1 Comfort-in-Noise (CIN)
compression clamp. Table V provides a consolidated summary of these
heuristics.

Crucially, a critical inspection reveals a profound \textbf{asymmetry in
evidentiary support} across these parameters. Several prominent
constants---most notably the 75\% air-bone-gap restoration rule and the
1.5:1 Comfort-in-Noise clamp---are pragmatic engineering choices without
direct empirical derivation. The 75\% ABG fraction, while widely adopted
in clinical prescriptive software (Johnson, 2013a) to avoid receiver
saturation and severe MPO clipping, has never been empirically
established against patient preference or behavioral speech recognition.
Similarly, the 1.5:1 CIN clamp is an asserted heuristic inspired by the
comfort-oriented design philosophy of NAL-NL3 (Kitterick et al., 2026),
but lacks independent perceptual validation. In sharp contrast, the
3.0:1 Compression Ratio (CR) upper ceiling enforced across the WDRC
stages and optimizer loss function represents the \textbf{single
best-supported constant in the framework}. This boundary is firmly
anchored in the extensive empirical psychoacoustic and speech-in-noise
literature by Pamela Souza and colleagues (Souza, 2002; Souza, Jenstad,
\& Boike, 2006), which demonstrates that wide dynamic range compression
ratios exceeding \textasciitilde3.0:1 cause severe temporal envelope
flattening, loss of acoustic contrast, and significant degradation of
speech perception in complex backgrounds. Explicitly documenting this
evidentiary asymmetry prevents readers from conflating robust
psychoacoustic limits with arbitrary engineering conveniences. Until
systematic multi-parameter sensitivity sweeps are conducted to map the
complex interactions of these boundaries, the theoretical outputs of
Open-NL remain inherently fragile approximations rather than robust
algorithmic conclusions.

\subsubsection{P. Multi-Parameter Sensitivity
Sweep}\label{p.-multi-parameter-sensitivity-sweep}

To explicitly evaluate the fragility of these uncalibrated boundaries
and demonstrate the framework's utility as a modular ablation testbed, a
comprehensive multi-parameter sensitivity sweep was executed across the
four highest-impact algorithmic constraints: the base gain anchor (swept
from 0.40 to 0.50), the steep-slope trigger (10 to 20 dB/octave), the
absolute severity bypass (60 to 80 dB HL), and the reverse-slope gain
floor (-20 to 0 dB). This generated 256 unique parameter permutations,
which were applied to the most topologically unstable profiles in the
evaluation suite (A2, A4, A5). Crucially, this sweep was purposefully
executed with the aggressive 60 dB HL severe-loss booster mode engaged,
rather than the conservative 70 dB HL default; otherwise, the resulting
extreme loudness excursions in A4 and A5 could be mistakenly
misattributed solely to the default configuration rather than their
interaction with the aggressive booster baseline.

The resulting variance (Table IV) provides a concrete, quantitative
demonstration of heuristic coupling, illustrating mechanistically why
unregularized optimization fails in practice and why algorithms like
NAL-NL2 required extensive empirical corrections (Keidser et al., 2012).
Rather than indicating solver failure, this variance reflects the acute
sensitivity of the objective landscape to uncalibrated heuristic
boundaries. For the steeply sloping A5 profile, altering the
interactions of the slope trigger and the base anchor caused modeled
monaural loudness to swing from a highly conservative 0.9 sones to a
potentially traumatic 5.3 sones, with theoretical intelligibility
(effective SII) fluctuating between 0.49 and 0.82. Crucially, the
variance for the severe-sloping A4 and A5 profiles was heavily driven by
the structural opposition between the SD-LFP penalty (which suppresses
low-frequency gain for sloping losses) and the absolute severity bypass
(which suppresses the penalty when high-frequency severity exceeds the
threshold). Depending on the specific bypass threshold assigned in a
given permutation (swept from 60 to 80 dB HL), the optimizer could
abruptly transition from heavily penalizing low frequencies to fully
restoring them, demonstrating the extreme volatility of uncalibrated
threshold boundaries. Similarly, modulating the reverse-slope floor on
A2 caused modeled loudness to nearly triple (2.7 to 8.4 sones). This
massive response envelope formally demonstrates that when operating
without explicit distortion penalties, the loudness-audibility
optimization surface is highly non-convex. However, this parameter
sensitivity is not a flaw of the testbed; rather, it is the primary
diagnostic utility that justifies the need for a principled
computational calibration framework. By exposing how the asserted
constants in Table V dictate the physiological safety of the output, the
sweep establishes the exact parameters that must be constrained by
distortion-aware objective functions prior to clinical evaluation.

\subsubsection{Q. Framework for Principled
Calibration}\label{q.-framework-for-principled-calibration}

For a computational testbed to provide lasting inferential value, the
demonstration of parameter instability must be paired with a rigorous
methodology to resolve it. While Open-NL ships with the uncalibrated
heuristic constants detailed in Table V to facilitate initial
exploration, the ultimate purpose of this open-source architecture is to
allow researchers to systematically calibrate these gates against
distortion-aware anchors. Rather than relying on massive, intractable
human clinical trials to blindly tune these interdependent parameters,
future research can explicitly utilize the Open-NL engine within a
global stochastic optimization grid (e.g., genetic algorithms or
particle swarm optimization). By evaluating targets against
comprehensive distortion-aware objective functions---such as the rigid
\texttt{johnson2011\_complete} clinical desensitization penalty or the
HASPI/HASQI metrics (Kates \& Arehart, 2022)---researchers can
systematically map the parameter space. To execute this, researchers can
construct an outer optimization loop that searches the multidimensional
space of heuristic constants (e.g., bounding \(G_{base}\) between
{[}0.3, 0.6{]}, the severe-loss booster onset between {[}50, 80{]} dB
HL, and the low-frequency penalty slope between {[}0, 0.5{]}). For each
proposed vector of constants, the inner Open-NL engine generates
discrete WDRC targets across a massive, representative corpus of
clinical audiograms (e.g., the NHANES dataset). These targets are then
passed to a time-domain acoustic simulation engine (such as openMHA) to
evaluate speech perception via HASPI/HASQI. The outer loop calculates a
global fitness score by maximizing population-level HASPI/HASQI while
strictly penalizing configurations that exceed physiological loudness
limits. Crucially, this population-level objective function must not
simply maximize mean performance, as doing so would structurally injure
the 40\% of patients residing in the excess-summation tail (Denk et al.,
2025). To prevent this, the outer optimizer must employ an asymmetric,
veto-based cost function: any proposed parameter vector that violates
the individualized broadband tolerance threshold (e.g., trueLOUDNESS)
for simulated outlier listeners must incur an overwhelming penalty. This
guarantees that the optimization routine's drive to maximize
population-wide intelligibility remains rigidly subordinated to
individual physiological safety. This nested architecture---an outer
stochastic search tuning the generalized ruleset, evaluated by an inner
deterministic optimization of the speech envelope---transforms the
derivation of prescriptive rules from a manual clinical art into a
reproducible computational science.

Crucially, this calibration roadmap must fundamentally abandon
generalized loudness predictions in favor of individualized
physiological safety limits. Currently, Open-NL relies on the Moore \&
Glasberg (2004) specific loudness engine, applying a standard
level-dependent 2--6 dB bilateral correction to account for binaural
loudness summation. However, as demonstrated by Denk et al.~(2025),
binaural broadband loudness summation in hearing-impaired listeners
averages \textasciitilde13 dB higher than in normal-hearing populations,
with \textasciitilde30--40\% of impaired listeners exceeding the
normal-hearing range entirely. Because this excess summation is highly
idiosyncratic---with individual variance spanning roughly -10 to +40
dB---and cannot be accurately predicted from the pure-tone audiogram or
narrowband loudness functions (Oetting et al., 2016, 2017), a static
2--6 dB correction constitutes a severe structural mismatch.

This mismatch partially undermines the use of generalized ``sones'' as a
definitive safety-defining outcome. If the U-shaped physiological
loudness ceilings governing Open-NL's objective function underestimate
actual perceived binaural loudness by \textasciitilde13 dB on average,
the resulting compression ratios will fail to protect a large minority
of listeners from catastrophic loudness rejection. Therefore, future
calibration of Open-NL's parameter space cannot rely purely on
stochastic optimization against theoretical metric baselines. The
theoretical safety ceilings must be replaced by individualized,
behaviorally measured broadband summation targets (such as those
employed in trueLOUDNESS paradigms; Oetting et al., 2017). By anchoring
the optimization engine to a patient's true binaural broadband tolerance
rather than an audiogram-derived generalized limit, the framework can
safely maximize intelligibility without violating idiosyncratic
physiological boundaries.

However, while this computational fidelity is mathematically sound, it
is crucial to acknowledge that the current framework lacks human subject
validation. Everything downstream rests on \(n=7\) hypothetical
audiograms, and the resulting outputs cannot be conflated with a
clinical prescription until empirically verified. Therefore, any future
clinical application of this framework must adhere to a strict,
non-negotiable validation protocol. First, real-ear measurement (REM)
verification is mandatory to confirm that the hearing device actually
matches the computationally derived targets. Empirical evidence robustly
demonstrates that REM-verified programmed fits significantly outperform
manufacturer first-fits on word/phoneme recognition and subjective
patient preference (Valente et al., 2018; Almufarrij, Munro, \& Dillon,
2020; Brockmeyer et al., 2021). Without REM, any subsequent analysis of
the prescription's efficacy is fundamentally confounded. Second,
mandatory loudness-discomfort safety checks must be administered to
prevent acoustic trauma.

Beyond safety protocols, this computational roadmap yields a concrete,
falsifiable clinical prediction: if the raw, uncalibrated Open-NL
targets---specifically the mathematically aggressive high-frequency
prescriptions for severe, steeply sloping profiles like A4 and A5---were
applied directly to human subjects, they would structurally trigger high
rates of loudness rejection. This prediction is quantitatively anchored
in existing behavioral evidence. As noted by Denk et al.~(2025), the
massive ±15 dB fine-tuning range historically required to reach
comfortable loudness in clinical trials (Keidser et al., 2012) directly
matches the theoretical spread between true binaural broadband summation
targets (trueLOUDNESS) and generic prescriptions like NAL-NL2. This
solidly supports the premise that targets exceeding standard generic
baselines are systematically rejected due to unmodeled, idiosyncratic
excess summation.

Because Open-NL's uncalibrated A4/A5 targets mathematically exceed
NAL-NL2 by roughly 11 dB at 4000 Hz, they push far beyond this
historical comfort boundary. To ensure this diagnostic failure mode is
formally testable, we offer the following operationally defined
hypothesis: If adult listeners with A4 or A5 audiometric profiles are
fitted with real-ear verified Open-NL targets, \textgreater80\% will
exhibit immediate categorical loudness rejection---operationally defined
as a rating of 6 (``Loud'') or 7 (``Uncomfortably Loud'') on the
standard 7-point Categorical Loudness Scaling (CLS) procedure (ISO
16832)---when presented with continuous broadband speech material (e.g.,
ISTS) at 65 dB SPL and 80 dB SPL inputs, relative to a strictly matched
NAL-NL2 clinical baseline. Systematically measuring this behavioral
rejection threshold will provide the essential empirical ground-truth
needed to validate and refine the distortion-aware penalty weights
required for future stochastic calibrations.

\subsection{III. EVALUATION AND TRADE-OFF
ANALYSIS}\label{iii.-evaluation-and-trade-off-analysis}

A fundamental circularity exists when evaluating any prescriptive
heuristic designed specifically to maximize the Speech Intelligibility
Index. Because Open-NL's gain shaping is tuned specifically to maximize
the effective SII metric, utilizing the \texttt{sii()} engine to compare
its targets against other validated prescriptions (like NAL-NL2) within
a simulated environment yields a tautological result rather than an
independent validation of clinical benefit. Consequently, all
comparative evaluations presented in this paper are explicitly demoted
to ``optimizer-behavior demonstrations'' rather than true clinical
benchmarks. Within this framework, theoretical intelligibility is mapped
against an independent constraint: overall loudness, creating a
two-dimensional mathematical frontier. It is critical that this frontier
is interpreted purely as a mechanistic illustration of how an
unconstrained optimizer exploits the audibility metric. Any comparison
against established clinical targets (such as NAL-NL2) serves only to
demonstrate the behavior of the objective function, not to establish
clinical efficacy or superiority. Furthermore, this analytical framework
is strictly bounded by its reliance on \(n=7\) hypothetical audiometric
profiles (Johnson \& Dillon, 2011). For reference, both NAL-NL2 and DSL
m{[}i/o{]} generally prescribe targets that yield \textasciitilde8 sones
for sensorineural losses at 65 dB SPL, compared to 18.6 sones for a
normal-hearing listener (Johnson \& Dillon, 2011). Table VI
quantitatively demonstrates that Open-NL's modeled loudness exceeds the
NAL-NL2 baseline in the majority of primary sensorineural test profiles
(A1, A2, and A3). Because Open-NL's targets meaningfully overshoot this
established loudness envelope of conservative adult prescriptions in
pursuit of effective SII maximization, these excursions illustrate the
aggressive nature of the abstract mathematical objective function rather
than any realizable clinical strategy.

Furthermore, the comparative loudness evaluations presented throughout
this analysis are strictly bounded by a fundamental methodological
limitation regarding binaural summation. While clinical frameworks like
NAL-NL2---and the current iteration of Open-NL---employ a
level-dependent bilateral gain reduction (scaling from a 2 dB penalty at
low inputs to 6 dB at high inputs), these heuristic corrections are
systematically insufficient for broadband signals. Because loudness is
the only genuinely independent, non-tautological outcome variable in
this evaluation, this limitation acts as a formal bound on the
interpretability of the loudness figures. As established by Oetting et
al.~(2016, 2017), narrowband loudness compensation systematically
over-amplifies binaural broadband signals, and the necessary correction
factor cannot simply be inferred from monaural narrowband functions.
This structural failure of narrowband models is compounded by massive
individual variability. Crucially, as Moore, Gibbs, and Onions (2014)
demonstrated, standard loudness models accurately predict narrowband and
diotic level differences for equal loudness (LDEL) within a tight
\textasciitilde4--6 dB envelope. Therefore, the excess summation
phenomenon is specifically a \emph{broadband, suprathreshold} effect
that the standard Moore \& Glasberg engine fails to capture, rather than
a uniform correction factor applicable to all signals. Denk et
al.~(2025) demonstrated that this specific broadband summation averages
\textasciitilde13 dB higher than normal in hearing-impaired listeners,
with approximately 40\% exceeding the normal range entirely. Utilizing
level-dependent 2--6 dB corrections while explicitly acknowledging this
+13 dB broadband error in 40\% of listeners renders the model's output
systematically anti-conservative. To bound this bias quantitatively:
because perceived loudness roughly doubles for every 10 dB increase in
signal intensity, an unmodeled +13 dB broadband excess summation
translates to a multiplicative factor of approximately 2.4\(\times\) in
the linear sone domain. Therefore, a theoretical target generating a
seemingly `safe' monaural mathematical prediction of 5.2 sones (e.g.,
profile A5) corresponds to an intolerable \textasciitilde12.4 sones of
perceived binaural loudness in listeners exhibiting this trait. To
expose this structural vulnerability directly, Table VI reports both the
canonical single-ear monaural loudness (maintaining direct comparability
with Johnson \& Dillon, 2011) and the modeled excess-summation binaural
loudness envelope. This explicitly proves why the loudness frontier must
be interpreted as strictly an illustrative computational benchmark
rather than a deployable clinical safety ceiling.

To facilitate this two-dimensional trade-off analysis natively within R,
the \texttt{SII} package implements a highly optimized C++ port of the
canonical Moore \& Glasberg (2004) specific-loudness model via
\texttt{Rcpp}, directly mirroring the exact logic of the
\texttt{bramslow2004} implementation in the Auditory Modeling Toolbox
(AMT; Majdak et al., 2022). This permits the evaluation of tens of
thousands of candidate gain curves without the prohibitive latency of
external MATLAB/Octave runtimes. To verify computational fidelity, the
final C++ Open-NL insertion gain targets were evaluated through a fully
independent time-domain synthesis pipeline using the AMT Octave engine.
The native C++ predictions closely matched the external time-domain AMT
outputs (\(r = 0.98\), Mean Absolute Error = 0.64 sones), mathematically
confirming that the internal C++ optimizer reliably reproduces the
canonical physiological model across all tested clinical profiles.

Using the embedded C++ engine, Open-NL was benchmarked against exact
insertion gain targets generated directly from the proprietary NAL-NL2
version 2 software (National Acoustic Laboratories, Sydney, Australia)
for soft (50 dB SPL), average (65 dB SPL), and loud (80 dB SPL)
conversational speech inputs. To ensure exact reproducibility of this
clinical baseline, all NAL-NL2 targets were explicitly extracted using
an 18-channel compression architecture with adaptive/dual time
constants, an occluded BTE with \#13 tubing acoustic coupling, and
assuming audiometric thresholds were measured via supra-aural
headphones. It must be explicitly stated that the fundamental evaluation
design employed here---converting insertion gains to eardrum output
across seven hypothetical adult audiograms to model Moore \& Glasberg
(2004) loudness alongside ANSI SII, and benchmarking these against
NAL-NL2---is a direct replication and expansion of the methodological
paradigm established by Johnson \& Dillon (2011). Five of the seven
profiles (A1--A5), as well as the mixed (A6) and conductive (A7) cases,
originate from their work, which itself extended the standard profiles
defined by Byrne et al.~(2001). The scientific novelty of the present
manuscript does not lie in the evaluation framework itself; rather, its
sole contribution is the open-source optimization and ablation layer
built \emph{on top} of this established Johnson \& Dillon (2011)
paradigm. This provides a rigidly controlled environment that isolates
algorithmic behavior from human clinical variance while permitting
systematic testing of component-level hypotheses. Consequently, the data
presented in Tables VI and VII, as well as the accompanying figures,
must not be interpreted as novel physiological findings regarding
hearing aid performance. Instead, they serve strictly as mechanistic
demonstrations of how an unconstrained, open-source Nelder-Mead
optimization engine behaves when forced to navigate the exact same
multidimensional evaluation space previously used to validate
closed-source clinical formulas.

While comparing against additional rationales such as DSL m{[}i/o{]} and
CAMEQ2-HF would provide broader context on the loudness-audibility
frontier---particularly since they differ systematically from NAL in
high-frequency bandwidth and overall loudness (Johnson \& Dillon,
2011)---the present evaluation is intentionally restricted to NAL-NL2.
Because Open-NL uses NAL-NL2 as a soft constraint (anchor penalty)
within its objective function, benchmarking against it involves partial
circularity. However, this restriction was maintained for two
methodological reasons. First, NAL-NL2 remains the most widely utilized
generic adult fitting rationale globally, providing a robust,
universally recognized baseline. Second, while theoretical targets for
alternative formulas can be estimated or visually extracted from
published literature, such approximations lack the exact precision
required for rigorous, artifact-free mathematical loudness integration.
Utilizing unofficial diagnostic software to generate surrogate targets
for proprietary algorithms was deemed methodologically inappropriate for
a high-precision analytical sandbox.

A crucial methodological boundary must be highlighted regarding the
evaluation metrics reported in Table VI. Because Open-NL directly
maximizes effective SII, reporting higher SII numbers for Open-NL
relative to NAL-NL2 is an unavoidable mathematical tautology: it merely
confirms that the numerical optimizer converged upon its defined
objective. Evaluating the resulting targets against ANSI SII alongside
desensitized SII does not provide an independent index of speech
intelligibility, as both metrics are mathematical variants of the same
underlying audibility model. In auditory modeling, independent,
distortion-aware speech perception evaluation is achieved through
waveform-level metrics such as the Hearing Aid Speech Perception Index
(HASPI) and Hearing Aid Speech Quality Index (HASQI; Kates \& Arehart,
2022). However, computing HASPI and HASQI requires a time-domain
acoustic simulation pipeline: convolving acoustic speech signals (.wav)
through a multi-channel dynamic range compressor, simulating cochlear
filterbank outputs, and calculating temporal envelope and fine-structure
cross-correlations. Because Open-NL operates strictly at the
prescriptive level---deriving frequency-gain insertion tables from
steady-state Long-Term Average Speech Spectra (LTASS) per Johnson \&
Dillon (2011)---it cannot natively evaluate waveform cross-correlations
without coupling to an external time-domain audio processor (such as
openMHA; Herzke et al., 2017). Consequently, HASPI and HASQI are
proposed herein as objective functions for future outer-loop audio
processing studies, rather than static prescriptive outcomes. Readers
must therefore interpret Table VI not as a claim of clinical superiority
or enhanced speech recognition, but strictly as an internal diagnostic
demonstration of how an unconstrained optimizer exploits the audibility
metric, and at what cost to physiological loudness and high-frequency
gain.

Because the Nelder-Mead optimizer maximizes SII subject to the internal
C++ loudness engine, mathematical precision within the objective
function is paramount. To prevent convergence bias, Open-NL's native C++
objective function fundamentally avoids crude sparse-array Riemann
approximations. Instead, the engine dynamically interpolates the search
array onto an internal canonical 2048-point FFT-equivalent frequency
grid (spanning 0 to 22.05 kHz). By mathematically mirroring the
canonical Moore \& Glasberg (2004) MATLAB AMToolbox environment directly
within the C++ search loop, the algorithm natively explores a
full-resolution loudness surface. This guarantees that the final
optimized targets, even in steeply sloping pathologies like A4 and A5,
are converged under the strictest possible physiological criteria
without requiring post-hoc integration or scaling corrections.

Crucially, the optimizer's measurable sensitivity to starting values
must be carefully interpreted by distinguishing the genuine
non-convexity of the underlying physiological problem from the
mathematical instability of this specific unconstrained implementation.
For steeply sloping profiles (e.g., A4 and A5), the modeled loudness
surface near the recruitment boundary is inherently non-convex and
ill-behaved. However, when the Open-NL framework navigates this space
using an unconstrained Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm against a hard,
non-smooth penalty constraint (the dynamic comfort cap), the optimizer
becomes acutely vulnerable to local minima, exacerbating the variance.
Evaluating the objective function landscape across two dimensions (e.g.,
sweeping 2 kHz and 4 kHz gain shifts for the A5 profile) confirms this
rugged topology, bounded by a sharp penalty cliff. Initializing with
varying flat shifts (e.g., -10 dB or +10 dB) across this landscape
yields final targets that deviate by up to 0.5 sones or 0.05 SII. This
structural fragility conclusively demonstrates that the concept of a
single, static ``optimal'' prescriptive target is inherently unstable
\emph{when generated by a pure mathematical optimizer lacking
regularization}. While the Open-NL software mechanically mitigates this
for reproducible ablation testing by utilizing a robust 5-iteration
multi-start routine (anchoring the baseline simplex with the NAL-R
target and executing four additional randomized iterations to extract
the global maximum---which reduces the residual run-to-run variance on
the A5 profile to \textless{} 0.05 sones and \textless{} 0.01 SII), the
deeper implication serves as a cautionary result. It is vital not to
over-generalize this variance as an intrinsic failure of all
prescriptive fitting. Clinical algorithms derived from the exact same
class of loudness and intelligibility models, such as NAL-NL2, yield
highly stable targets precisely because their optimization is strictly
bounded and empirically regularized using real-world preference data and
smooth, continuous objective gradients (Keidser, Dillon, Carter, \&
O'Brien, 2012). By removing those bounds, Open-NL illustrates the
mathematical chaos that occurs when necessary clinical regularizations
are stripped away, proving that uncalibrated static targets generated by
pure optimization must be viewed with skepticism absent rigorous
behavioral constraints.

It is critical to evaluate these results strictly through the lens of
the loudness frontier rather than raw SII maximization, while
recognizing that this analytical framework is inherently bounded by its
reliance on \(n=7\) hypothetical ears rather than clinical subjects.
Furthermore, the A7 profile (representing a pure conductive loss with a
50 dB air-bone gap) perfectly illustrates the engine's ability to model
mechanical middle-ear attenuation directly as an in-line mathematical
dampener within the C++ cochlear excitation loop. By enforcing a strict
75\% restoration limit (Section II.K), Open-NL safely prescribes 37.5 dB
of linear insertion gain for this profile. Because the Nelder-Mead
optimizer evaluates the baseline heuristic score to seed its
\texttt{best\_score} prior to search, it correctly recognizes that the
pure 37.5 dB linear gain produces a mathematically perfect output (1.00
SII) and avoids wandering into worse solutions on the flat, saturated
objective surface. The internal optimizer's dynamic cap further
constrains the output to modeled physiological boundaries, resulting in
a theoretical target yielding a mathematical output of 1.00 SII at a
canonical 3.85 sones. While this is slightly louder than NAL-NL2's
baseline (which outputs 3.10 sones), it reflects the direct mathematical
consequence of the chosen restoration fraction. Crucially, this 75\% ABG
restoration rule is an engineering choice---adapted from clinical
conventions (such as DSL v5.0; Scollie et al., 2005, and Johnson, 2013a)
to prevent receiver saturation and severe MPO distortion---rather than
an empirically derived preference optimum. The 75\% restoration rule has
never been directly validated against behavioral listener preference or
speech-recognition performance. This contrasts sharply with
well-anchored parameters like the 3.0:1 Compression Ratio ceiling (Stage
12), which is directly grounded in extensive empirical psychoacoustic
data on temporal envelope distortion and speech-in-noise deficits
(Souza, 2002; Souza, Jenstad, \& Boike, 2006). Acknowledging this
asymmetry in evidentiary support makes clear that the near-ceiling SII
of A7 is a structural artifact of an asserted engineering convention,
rather than a preference-validated clinical optimum.

Because Open-NL directly optimizes the mathematically smoothed Johnson

For extreme steeply sloping or profound losses (A4, A5), unmodified SII
maximization inherently attempts to push massive high-frequency gain.
While Open-NL integrates the Johnson and Dillon (2011) desensitization
penalty to mathematically temper this drive, a critical tension emerges
in the net behavior: as detailed in Table VII, Open-NL still prescribes
substantially more high-frequency gain than NAL-NL2 for these steeply
sloping profiles (e.g., +10.9 dB at 4000 Hz for A4, and +11.7 dB for A5
at 65 dB SPL inputs). If this aggressive behavior is evaluated strictly
against severe, confirmed dead-region pathologies, it represents a
failure. As established by Ching, Dillon, and Byrne (1998) and Baer,
Moore, and Kluk (2002), listeners with extensive, confirmed dead regions
gain no benefit from amplified audibility above
\textasciitilde1.5--2\(\times\) the edge frequency, and amplification in
these specific ears should target low or zero sensation levels.

However, assuming that Open-NL's objective function simply needs a
``heavier'' penalty to forcibly suppress A4/A5 targets misreads the
broader clinical evidence. A blanket, audiogram-driven high-frequency
suppression logic would systematically under-amplify the majority of
candidates who actually benefit from audibility. Cox et al.~(2011, 2012)
demonstrated that while roughly one-third of typical adult candidates
test positive for dead regions, standard high-frequency gain never
produced poorer performance, and audibility remained helpful on average
regardless of dead-region status. Pepler et al.~(2015) further confirmed
that even listeners with restricted high-frequency dead regions show
reduced---but not absent---benefit in noise. Therefore, enforcing a
heavier, blanket desensitization penalty solely based on the pure-tone
audiogram risks harming the majority of patients. This tension confirms
that severe-loss penalties cannot be generalized from the audiogram
alone; they must be dynamically tied to confirmed dead-region
diagnostics (e.g., TEN tests). Furthermore, as Engler, Digeser, and
Hoppe (2026) demonstrated, aided speech recognition is practically
insufficient in most ears above 80 dB HL regardless of prescribed gain,
arguing against treating the massive theoretical SII targets in A4/A5 as
clinically meaningful. This structural tension directly foregrounds the
framework's primary falsifiable prediction (Section II.Q): the
mathematically aggressive, uncalibrated A4/A5 high-frequency targets
will predictably trigger \textgreater80\% loudness rejection if deployed
on human subjects, proving that individualized distortion-aware
objective functions are required for severe losses.

Furthermore, for conductive and mixed losses (A6, A7), the optimizer
elegantly separates the mechanical attenuation of the middle ear from
sensorineural cochlear damage, strictly restricting the desensitization
penalty solely to the sensorineural component. Open-NL seamlessly
compensates for the linear attenuation in A7 by applying a 75\% air-bone
gap (ABG) restoration rule across all frequencies and input levels
(prescribing an exact, flat 37.5 dB of linear gain for 50, 65, and 80 dB
inputs without triggering sensorineural compression). While the
structural separation of conductive mechanics from sensorineural
distortion is physically sound, the specific 75\% restoration scalar
remains an uncalibrated engineering choice that directly dictates the
resulting audibility scores, rather than a preference-validated optimum.
This clean handling of conductive mechanics serves as a positive
demonstration of the modular architecture, while highlighting the need
for empirical calibration against behavioral listener data.

Similarly, under the active inclusion of reverse-slope compensation
physics via the SD-LFP constraint, the heuristic output for the mild
reverse-slope loss (A2) mathematically restricts unbounded loudness
growth. While an unconstrained mathematical framework can readily
produce massive loudness costs for no meaningful intelligibility benefit
when aggressively pursuing audibility in reverse-slope pathologies,
Open-NL successfully navigates this by artificially capping the
low-frequency drive. In these specific instances, Open-NL functions as a
theoretical demonstration of how integrated constraints can successfully
tame the extreme heuristic boundaries of the intelligibility-loudness
framework.

It must be explicitly acknowledged that the evaluation scope presented
herein---testing seven hypothetical audiograms by a single author
without human subjects---is strictly demonstrative. This narrow
evaluation cannot support any generalizable claim regarding clinical
superiority or patient preference. Open-NL is exclusively an open-source
computational testbed for researchers to modify and model isolated
prescriptive rules. Any extrapolation of these derived targets to human
patients requires independent institutional review, comprehensive
real-ear verification, and rigorous behavioral validation.

To ensure the computational fidelity of this evaluation framework, the
fast C++ approximation of the Moore \& Glasberg (2004) loudness model
utilized internally by the optimizer was formally validated against the
canonical Octave Bramslow (2004) Auditory Modeling Toolbox (AMT) engine.
For the purely sensorineural profiles (A1-A5), the fast C++
approximation closely mirrored the canonical physiological filterbank
model, validating the implementation of the non-linear Outer and Inner
Hair Cell (OHC/IHC) damage mechanics. However, direct numerical
comparison against the AMT wrapper was intentionally omitted for the
mixed and conductive profiles (A6 and A7). The canonical
\texttt{bramslow2004} AMT implementation was designed strictly for
sensorineural losses and lacks a native parameter for the Air-Bone Gap,
forcing any conductive component to be mathematically misclassified as
sensorineural hair-cell damage. Our C++ framework extends the
physiological evaluation by natively separating the Air-Bone Gap as a
linear pre-cochlear attenuator---faithfully executing Moore and
Glasberg's theoretical guidelines for conductive pathologies.
Consequently, while the novel C++ physiological sones are reported for
A6 and A7 to demonstrate the optimizer's robust handling of mixed
losses, they are explicitly not benchmarked against the structurally
limited AMT wrapper. Crucially, as established by the comparative data
of Johnson and Dillon (2011, 2013) and Ching, Dillon, and Byrne (1998),
maximizing the raw physical-audibility ANSI SII metric does not
straightforwardly equate to maximized clinical benefit. While audibility
is a powerful predictor of speech recognition in quiet, it explains
progressively less variance as loss worsens. In listeners with severe
loss, the magnitude of the hearing loss, reduced spectral resolution,
and the number of cochlear dead regions account for roughly comparable
shares of speech-in-quiet variance, with dead regions specifically
blunting the benefit of restored high-frequency audibility (Vestergaard,
2003; Souza et al., 2018; Moualed, Humphries, \& Ramsden, 2018).
Empirically, aided speech recognition becomes insufficient in most ears
above \textasciitilde80 dB HL regardless of how aggressively targets are
met (Engler, Digeser, \& Hoppe, 2026). If an optimization framework
structurally optimizes for the raw ANSI SII metric, its highest-scoring
targets drastically over-predict the usefulness of amplified regions in
severely impaired ears. High amounts of prescribed gain may increase
physical audibility on paper but severely degrade true perceptual
clarity due to unmodeled suprathreshold distortion (Margolis et al.,
2025).

To reconcile this limitation, Open-NL explicitly shifts its objective
function from physical audibility to the `effective audibility'
framework pioneered by Ching, Dillon, \& Byrne (1998) and Ching et
al.~(2001). The core theoretical premise of effective audibility
dictates that beyond a certain degree of sensorineural impairment,
increasing physical audibility yields diminishing or even negative
returns for speech recognition due to extreme cochlear distortion at
high sensation levels. Importantly, while historical models often
attributed this high-frequency roll-off entirely to ``cochlear dead
regions,'' this computational framework requires a more nuanced
physiological justification. As Cox et al.~(2012) demonstrated,
approximately 31\% of typical moderate-to-severe hearing aid candidates
test positive for dead regions, yet providing high-frequency audibility
to these ears still improved speech recognition on average regardless of
their dead-region status. Consequently, Open-NL's mathematical
desensitization module is not justified by a blanket dead-region
diagnosis, but rather by the structural sensation-level constraints
established by Ching, Dillon, \& Byrne (1998) and Baer, Moore, \& Kluk
(2002): for profound thresholds, the sheer magnitude of required gain
generates prohibitive distortion and diminishing returns irrespective of
discrete hair-cell survival. To model this, Open-NL implements a
bifurcated desensitization framework adapted from the empirically
derived severe-loss desensitization formula of Johnson \& Dillon (2011),
which mathematically penalizes the raw SII band-importance functions in
regions of profound loss. Crucially, the internal engine applies this
desensitization penalty using strictly the \emph{sensorineural}
threshold (\(T'_i - J_i\)). Previous computational models often applied
desensitization based on the total hearing threshold, falsely penalizing
conductive components as if they caused cochlear dead regions. By
correctly separating the mechanical attenuation of the middle ear from
cochlear damage, the model preserves high SII scores for conductive
losses while properly penalizing severe sensorineural cases.
Furthermore, to ensure strict physiological accuracy, Open-NL corrects a
fundamental architectural flaw in the standard programmatic
implementation of the ANSI S3.5 internal noise floor (\(X'_i\)). While
historical software modeled \(X'_i\) using the total hearing threshold
(\(X'_i = X_i + T'_i\)), Open-NL restricts this calculation strictly to
the sensorineural threshold (\(X'_i = X_i + \max(0, T'_i - J_i)\)). This
explicitly prevents conductive losses from artificially inflating the
internal cochlear noise floor, a physical impossibility.

To implement this desensitization, Open-NL first isolates the pure
sensorineural component of the hearing threshold by subtracting the
conductive air-bone gap (\(T_{hl} = \max(0, T'_i - J_i)\)). It then
computes the base desensitization multiplier (\(m\)) and the asymptotic
exponent (\(p\)):

\[ m = \frac{1}{1 + e^{0.075(T_{hl} - 66)}} \]
\[ p = \frac{T_{hl}}{8} - 15 \]

The rigid clinical formula (\texttt{johnson2011\_complete}) applies
these variables to the raw audibility index (\(K_i\)) using an
asymptotic exponentiation:

\[ K'_{complete} = (K_i^p + m^p)^{1/p}, \]

where \(T_{hl}\) is the isolated sensorineural hearing threshold (dB
HL), \(T'_i\) is the total equivalent hearing threshold level for
frequency band \(i\), \(J_i\) is the mechanical air-bone gap (conductive
attenuation) for band \(i\), \(m\) is the base desensitization
multiplier, \(p\) is the asymptotic exponent controlling the severity of
the penalty, \(K_i\) is the raw band audibility function (the proportion
of the speech dynamic range exceeding the noise floor prior to any
severe-loss desensitization penalties), and \(K'_{complete}\) is the
rigidly penalized clinical band audibility function.

Because this strict formulation---alongside piecewise limits on the
noise floor---creates severe topological discontinuities
(zero-gradients) that stall unconstrained numerical optimizers like
Nelder-Mead, the internal optimizer evaluates intermediate targets
against a continuous mathematical relaxation
(\texttt{johnson2011\_smoothed}). This smoothed heuristic safely anchors
the optimization engine by applying the multiplier as a simple linear
scale:

\[ K'_{smoothed} = K_i \cdot m, \]

where \(K'_{smoothed}\) is the continuous mathematical relaxation used
by the optimizer to maintain fluid gradient descent.

This permits fluid gradient descent without trapping the algorithm in
artificial local minima or numerical \texttt{Inf} bounds. Once the
insertion gain targets are finalized by the optimizer, the resulting
metrics are rigorously post-scored against the rigid clinical
\(K'_{complete}\) formula. As demonstrated in Table VI, this finalized
clinical penalty correctly mitigates the raw ANSI audibility for
severe/profound profiles (such as aggressively penalizing A5 from 0.68
to 0.62), thereby structurally aligning the theoretical optimization
outputs with realistic clinical expectations for profound sensorineural
distortion. However, consistent with the overarching framework of the
Open-NL testbed, it must be explicitly noted that the magnitude of this
desensitization penalty is itself an unvalidated heuristic constant (now
formally consolidated in Table V). Because the entire premise of this
computational architecture is the interrogation of uncalibrated
heuristics, this specific mathematical penalty must be treated with the
same scientific skepticism as the gain anchors it suppresses, pending
systematic sensitivity mapping.

\subsubsection{A. Numerical Convergence Stability vs.~Heuristic
Parameter
Sensitivity}\label{a.-numerical-convergence-stability-vs.-heuristic-parameter-sensitivity}

To prevent misinterpretation, a sharp conceptual distinction must be
maintained between \textbf{heuristic parameter sensitivity} (how
prescriptive targets respond when clinical rules and boundaries are
altered, as demonstrated in Section II.P) and \textbf{numerical
convergence stability} (the mathematical solver's ability to reliably
reach the same global optimum once a specific ruleset is fixed). The
headline fragility demonstrated in the parameter sweep is an intrinsic
property of uncalibrated heuristic rule interactions, \emph{not} a
failure of numerical optimization. Indeed, a major vulnerability of
non-linear simplex solvers like Nelder-Mead in high-dimensional
audiological spaces is algorithmic brittleness---the potential to stall
in shallow local minima or produce chaotic targets simply due to random
initialization seeds.

To systematically verify that the Nelder-Mead engine achieves genuine
mathematical convergence independent of starting conditions, a 768-point
Monte Carlo permutation analysis was conducted across the 5-start
regularization routine (Figure 4). While the 256-point sweep in Section
II.P proved that modifying the clinical rules radically shifts the
objective landscape, this 768-point Monte Carlo analysis tests whether
the solver reliably solves the \emph{same} fixed rules when its
mathematical hyperparameters (initial simplex dimensions, reflection
coefficients, and starting seed jitter) are varied across their
operational bounds for three discrete clinical profiles (A2, A4, A5).

As demonstrated in Figure 4, despite wide variations in algorithmic
hyperparameters and initialization seeds, the optimization engine
exhibited exceptional numerical convergence stability. Across all 768
permutations, the standard deviation of the final ANSI SII metric was
less than 0.025 for all tested profiles, and the resulting physiological
loudness variance was tightly clustered (\(\text{SD} < 0.05\) sones for
the profound A4 and A5 configurations). These narrow distributions
confirm that the Open-NL engine is mathematically stable: the
multi-start Nelder-Mead simplex reliably locates the global optimum on
the defined objective surface. Consequently, the wide target swings
observed across clinical profiles in Section II.P are definitively
attributable to the design of the heuristic rules themselves, rather
than solver stochasticity.

\emph{Figure 4. Distribution of resulting ANSI SII scores and
physiological loudness penalties across 768 permutations (256 \(\times\)

However, it is crucial to recognize that this exceptional numerical
stability---achieved via multi-start regularization against rigid
mathematical audiograms---almost certainly overstates the algorithm's
real-world clinical robustness. As demonstrated by Dao et al.~(2021),
physical real-ear-to-coupler differences (RECD) and acoustic coupling
leakages cause real-world fittings to deviate most severely from
theoretical targets precisely at thresholds above 85 dB HL and in
steeply sloping configurations. Because Open-NL's objective function is
most volatile exactly in this regime (requiring aggressive multi-start
regularization to stabilize the A4 and A5 profiles), translating these
theoretical targets to human ear canals will introduce physical variance
that is entirely invisible to the current synthetic evaluation space.
Consequently, achieving a stable numerical maximum (SD \textless{} 0.025
SII) on a 7-profile mathematical testbed must not be misconstrued as
physical reliability.

\subsubsection{B. Proposal for Minimal Clinical
Validation}\label{b.-proposal-for-minimal-clinical-validation}

While model-on-model evaluations computationally verify that the
algorithm behaves as mathematically intended, they fundamentally cannot
establish clinical utility. To bridge this gap, any minimal validation
study of the Open-NL framework must mandate specific, non-negotiable
hard gating requirements to protect subjects and ensure data integrity.
First, because physical fit-to-target variance introduces acoustic
deviations that computationally generated targets cannot foresee (Dao et
al., 2021), any behavioral trial must mandate Real-Ear Measurement (REM)
verification as a hard gate. As established by Valente et al.~(2018),
subjective outcomes are inextricably tied to precise acoustic matches in
the ear canal; failing to achieve a verified real-ear match invalidates
the subsequent behavioral data. Second, given the systemic
over-amplification risk posed by the uncalibrated severe-loss
heuristics, individualized Uncomfortable Loudness Level (UCL) or
trueLOUDNESS tolerance measurement must be mandated as a strict
behavioral safety gate prior to any speech testing. Finally, the study
must establish a robust clinical control. Mueller (2005) demonstrated a
lack of evidence supporting gain levels exceeding traditional NAL
formulations for severe losses, and contemporary data from Engler,
Digeser, and Hoppe (2026) explicitly favors conservative prescriptions
(such as DSL v5.0) in the 50--80 dB HL range. Because Open-NL's
highest-SII targets mathematically exceed these conservative baselines
in severe profiles, a minimal validation study should structurally
hypothesize that these extreme targets will trigger loudness rejection.
By formally measuring this behavioral rejection boundary against a
real-ear matched NAL-NL2 or DSL baseline, researchers can empirically
constrain the necessary distortion penalties required to safely bind the
optimizer in human subjects.

\subsection{V. CONCLUSION}\label{v.-conclusion}

Open-NL provides a transparent, customizable computational framework for
predicting WDRC insertion gain targets and benchmarking them against
established industry standards natively within R. By coupling an
explicitly defined mathematical pipeline with an embedded computational
loudness model, the \texttt{SII} package allows audiologists and
researchers to freely simulate and evaluate the theoretical
intelligibility-loudness trade-off of various prescriptive heuristics
without relying on opaque clinical fitting software. As the framework
matures, it must eventually accommodate forthcoming prior art such as
NAL-NL3 (Kitterick, Zakis, \& Edwards, 2026). Because the NAL philosophy
and its modular successor represent the established clinical standard,
they are the natural comparators against which an open prescriptive
substrate must be evaluated. It is critically important to emphasize
that Open-NL is currently a computational toolkit designed exclusively
for theoretical modeling and simulation. It has not undergone clinical
validation and is strictly contraindicated for general clinical fitting.
Given the inclusion of exploratory features like the severe-loss booster
and dynamic MPO attenuation, applying these raw targets to human
subjects carries an inherent risk of over-amplification or discomfort.
Any future application of Open-NL targets to human subjects must occur
exclusively within the strictly controlled context of Institutional
Review Board (IRB) approved research studies, and mandates independent
real-ear verification and rigorous loudness-discomfort safety checks.
